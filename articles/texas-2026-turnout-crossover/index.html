Candidate votes are not party registration or voter histories. Changes may reflect turnout, persuasion, different nominees, third-party votes and ballot roll-off.

One election, different voter behavior

In the 2022 governor race, Greg Abbott received 4,437,099 votes and Beto O’Rourke 3,553,656.

In the 2026 governor race, unofficial totals showed Abbott with 4,524,527 and Gina Hinojosa with 5,230,789.

That is roughly 1.68 million additional votes for the Democratic candidate, while the Republican candidate’s total was roughly 87,000 higher than in 2022. It is therefore inaccurate to explain the statewide result solely as Republicans staying home.

One surprising Senate comparison

James Talarico’s 2026 total exceeded Beto O’Rourke’s 2018 Senate vote by roughly 1.4 million votes, even though both were midterms.

05 / Geographic shifts

Where did participation rise the most?

County-level changes are powerful evidence of a changing electorate, but they do not establish whether particular residents switched their votes. Compare regional turnout-rate changes from 2022.

Change in turnout rate, 2022 → 2026

Which areas moved?

Border counties: The 16-county border region saw turnout rise more than 8 percentage points. Several counties that supported Trump in 2024 swung sharply back toward Democrats in the 2026 Senate election.

Suburbs: Collin, Denton and Fort Bend were among the places with turnout growth around 4.7 points. Talarico narrowly carried the Tribune’s suburban grouping after Ted Cruz had won it by about 19 points in 2018.

Rural Texas: Turnout increased roughly 1.4 points, while the Republican Senate margin in rural counties declined from approximately 54.4 points in 2018 to 45 points in 2026.

Some counties recorded declining turnout and some growth. A shift in county margins is not proof that any particular voter changed candidates.

06 / What do we know about three-time Trump voters?

“I voted for Trump three times. In 2026, I voted for a Democrat.”

Firsthand anecdotal evidence

Yes, that exact pattern can occur.

The author personally knows more than one person who says they voted for Donald Trump in 2016, 2020 and 2024, then supported a Democrat in the 2026 midterms.

These firsthand accounts are worth recording, because they describe a more specific form of crossover than simply “voted for Trump in 2024.” They are self-reported anecdotes, not independent ballot verification or a representative sample.

What surveys can and cannot establish

The frequency remains unknown.

Voter surveys showing approximately one-tenth of 2024 Trump voters supporting some 2026 Democratic Senate candidates give statistical context for switching, including in Texas. They do not isolate everyone who supported Trump in all three presidential elections.

There is no reliable population-wide percentage for that precise 3× Trump-to-Democrat sequence in the evidence reviewed here. The anecdotes establish reported examples—not an estimate of how many such Texans exist.

Three things can all be true at once

Republican-leaning voters may sit out; some voters may cross party lines; and Democratic-leaning or previously absent voters may turn out. Official totals alone cannot partition the electorate into these individual histories. The best assessment combines returns, registration data and well-designed voter surveys—without treating them as interchangeable.

Methods & limitations

Texas turnout denominator: total ballots divided by the Texas Secretary of State’s registered-voter count. The 2026 rate is calculated from a Nov. 6 unofficial governor-race vote count and an October 16 registration announcement. Some ballots omit a vote for governor, so the calculated value is a conservative proxy—not the eventual certified ballots-cast turnout statistic.

National turnout denominator: voting-eligible population (VEP) per University of Florida Election Lab, not total population or registered voters. Its preliminary estimate for the total Texas 2026 ballot count is 10.365 million (48.82% of eligible voters), which differs from the narrower official governor-race vote total of 9.907 million displayed in Texas calculations here. These use different measurements and should not be spliced into one series without checking methodology and timestamps.

Voting-preference data: candidate vote totals, self-reported party affiliation, recalled 2024 presidential votes and county-level swings measure different things. Survey-based person-count extrapolations assume the sample percentages apply to the election-wide ballot count and are displayed rounded.

Changes over time: late ballots, recounts, corrections, and certified canvasses may alter the preliminary 2026 figures. No demographic or geographic comparison proves why an individual voted a particular way.
