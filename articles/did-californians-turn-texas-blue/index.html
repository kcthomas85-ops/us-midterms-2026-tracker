Did California turn Texas blue?

The claim

“Californians moved in and turned Texas blue.”

It's a familiar political talking point. And with Texas's historic Democratic victories in 2026, it's making the rounds again.

The reality

That explanation doesn't hold up to the data.

Historical exit polls and research on California migrants tell a much more interesting—and much less convenient—story.

Yes, Democrats won major statewide races in Texas in 2026. No, that doesn't prove people moving from California caused it. Let's look at what we actually know.

01 / The plot twist

Who was voting blue?
Texas-born voters.

In two elections, people born in Texas favored the Democrat for U.S. Senate. Voters born elsewhere favored the Republican. Tap between election years to compare the margins.

03 / Follow the movers

Okay, but what about
Californians specifically?

Researchers James Gimpel and Daron Shaw actually tracked California-to-Texas movers. Their conclusion: the migration was politically mixed, and its statewide partisan effect was small.

In many Texas communities, more registered Republicans than Democrats were arriving from California. And migrants tended to settle among people with similar politics.

Williamson County, near Austin, makes the point beautifully: it was becoming more competitive for Democrats even as the identified Californians moving there leaned Republican.

  • Migration can change individual neighborhoods.
  • That doesn't mean it changes the whole state's political balance.
  • California is not one big, politically uniform voting bloc.

04 / They're not all liberal

The California exodus
has a red streak.

The Public Policy Institute of California analyzed registered voters who moved out of California between the 2020 and 2024 presidential elections. Republicans were overrepresented among those leaving.

Republican share of California departures39%
Republican share of California electorate25%

This is a comparison of Republican shares, not a claim that 61% of leavers were Democrats, or that every leaver moved to Texas. PPIC's research ↗

★ Why leave?

Taxes? Possibly. Housing? Definitely a factor.

Texas has no state individual income tax, but Californians also move for housing costs, jobs, family and lifestyle. And Texans still face local property and sales taxes.

The research doesn't show that all migrants left to escape taxes—or that lower taxes determine their vote.

06 / So what actually changed?

Texas had its own
reasons to vote differently.

There's no magic single-cause explanation for a statewide election. These are four important pieces of the bigger picture.

✦

Generational shifts

Younger Texans aren't politically identical to their parents and grandparents. New generations can reshape the electorate from within.

▦

Growing suburbs

Dallas–Fort Worth, Houston, Austin and San Antonio suburbs have grown, diversified and become central to statewide races.

↗

National backlash

Texas's Democratic victories came amid a wider 2026 backlash against Trump and Republican policies, not in isolation.

★

Candidate choices

Individual candidates matter, too. Voters can reject specific leaders or policies without permanently changing party loyalty.

Further reading: Texas Tribune's suburban voting analysis · AP's November 2026 election analysis.

What we cannot count: Neither certified ballots nor county results identify the birthplace of voters for James Talarico or Gina Hinojosa. Without a suitable 2026 survey, no one can truthfully give the exact number of Texas-born people who voted for either candidate. Historical 2018 and 2024 exit polls should not be passed off as 2026 vote counts.

07 / The bottom line

Texas didn't have to become California to vote for change.

Does migration affect politics? Of course. Can California-born voters influence particular districts? Absolutely. But claiming they caused Texas's 2026 blue wave needs evidence that hasn't been established.

The historical evidence points toward something rather different: in the last two Cruz Senate elections, native Texans voted more Democratic than newcomers.

“Stop blaming Californians for how Texas voted. Maybe start listening to Texans.”
