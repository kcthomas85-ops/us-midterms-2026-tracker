Texas / Divided does not mean powerless

Yes, Texas can still get things done.

Republican Senate control makes some changes harder. It still leaves several routes to meaningful action: enforcing existing laws, bargaining over legislation and spending, and using the House or governor to stop proposals. These are powers to use—not a prediction that every official will use them in the same way.

Existing authority

Act without passing a new bill

The attorney general can set lawful consumer-protection priorities, investigate complaints and bring civil cases. That could mean a different emphasis on scams, deceptive practices or other violations already covered by law.

The governor can direct functions legally assigned to the office and propose appointments as vacancies arise. Many agencies have separate boards or elected leaders, and many appointments need Senate confirmation.

Practical benefit: existing protections can matter more if enforcement improves. Agency implementation can change within the authority and budget already provided.

Senate agreement needed

Make a deal that changes policy

The House, Senate and governor all have a role in passing a budget. House Democrats can seek more school funding, different voucher allocations or other priorities as part of negotiations. Neither chamber can enact the other’s preferred budget alone.

Bills with cross-party support can also pass. The workable coalition depends on the proposal, committee decisions and the Senate’s rules.

Practical benefit: a negotiated appropriation or narrower reform can produce real services even without agreement on a full party agenda.

House / governor check

Stop or renegotiate a proposal

A Senate bill still needs House passage. The governor can veto legislation, subject to the constitutional override process. Those checks can preserve an existing protection or force a different compromise.

A Democratic lieutenant governor may have influence over committees and the calendar. Senators can change those procedural powers, so the office’s title alone does not guarantee control.

Practical benefit: blocking a damaging change can protect people immediately, while negotiations may secure a more useful bill.

Where the new power could be used

What can still stop the change?

Control is not enactment

Congress is not the same as state governmentA federal presidential veto cannot stop an ordinary state statute, but federal statutes, constitutional rights, judicial rulings, state constitutions and available funding still constrain state policy.
Trifecta

Same party controls governor + both chambers. Ordinary state bills have a clearer path to becoming law, but agreement and legal limits are still needed.

Divided government

Different parties control key offices. Passing a new law often needs negotiation, but either chamber or governor may block proposals.

Independent office / commission

Attorneys general, education boards, courts and regulators may exercise powers through lawsuits, rules, contracts or decisions without passing a new statute.

07 / The election after this election

Why the 2026 states matter for 2028.

Ballot-processing timelines

Democratic trifectas in Pennsylvania and Wisconsin could consider pre-processing rules for mail ballots. Earlier processing could affect when unofficial results become available in 2028.

Any bill requires legislative passage, gubernatorial approval and compliance with applicable law.

Source ↗

Secretaries of state

Georgia, Ohio and Indiana elected Democratic secretaries of state according to the November 7 reporting. Their election-administration responsibilities matter, but authority differs by state and is bounded by law.

Secretaries cannot unilaterally rewrite statutes or decide the presidential result.

Source ↗

Attorneys general & courts

New attorneys general may change litigation priorities and how their states defend or challenge election rules. Courts continue to review whether changes are lawful.

The office cannot simply repeal a statute on its own.

Reuters ↗
Important distinction: election administration ≠ control of the result. Election laws, independent courts, constitutional safeguards and federal protections continue to apply. Neither a state secretary of state nor a governor can lawfully change vote totals at will.

Methods & limitations

Chamber flips: NCSL's November 6 report is the principal list, including Minnesota's tied-to-Democratic transition. Arizona and Georgia are included with the caveat that some seats are unofficial. A separate November 6 NCSL interview cites 13 changes, while the named-chamber overview lists 12. This page uses the explicit 12-chamber list rather than presenting either tally as a certified final total.

State control: Gov + two chambers are shown separately; a Democratic lieutenant governor, regulator, attorney general or court seat is not proof of a Democratic lawmaking trifecta.

2027 priorities: announced goals, earlier vetoed bills and our analysis of institutional opportunities are labeled separately. Dates identify stages to monitor, not promised enactment deadlines. The 13 focus states are not an exhaustive inventory of local wins.

Policy scenarios: examples illustrate powers and proposed reforms, not enacted legislation, endorsements, or forecasts. Budget restrictions, courts, federal preemption and procedural rules all matter.

Texas State Board of Education: the 8–7 hypothetical depends on District 8, whose lead was under 600 votes in the available November 5 update. No definitive flip is asserted. For New Hampshire, the House/Senate figures are unofficial election tallies subject to certification and recounts.
